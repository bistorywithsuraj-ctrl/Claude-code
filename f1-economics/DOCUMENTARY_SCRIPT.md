# HOW FORMULA 1 LEARNED TO PRINT MONEY
### A Greenback documentary · ~15 minutes · Vox-style

**Format key**
- **VO** = narration (≈145 words/min).
- **[CLIP]** = a real interview or archive moment to source. Lines marked "paraphrase" describe what the person says and must be replaced with their exact words from the source clip. Never invent a quote.
- **[CUTOUT]** = a real photo, background removed, with a white paper edge, drop shadow and slight parallax.
- **[DOC]** = a real document, headline or filing on paper texture, with a highlighter pass.
- **[GFX]** = animated infographic.
- **[VERIFY]** = confirm the figure before recording.

## COLD OPEN: "THE LAST RACE" (0:00 – 1:30)

**[ARCHIVE]** Monza, September 2020. An empty grandstand (COVID year). A Williams car rolls into the pit lane.

**VO:** On a Sunday in September 2020, at Monza in Italy, a team principal walked out of the Formula 1 paddock for the last time.

**[CUTOUT]** Claire Williams, isolated on a black-and-white paper texture, slides in from the left. Behind her, a halftone of Sir Frank Williams from the 1980s fades up.

**VO:** Her name was Claire Williams. Her father, Frank, had started the team in his twenties and built it into one of the most successful teams in the history of the sport: nine constructors' championships and seven drivers' titles.

**[GFX]** A trophy shelf counts up: 9 constructors' 🏆 and 7 drivers' 🏆. The shelf then collapses and slides off.

**VO:** But by 2020 Williams was finishing last almost every weekend. It had lost its title sponsor. It was losing money. So the family did the only thing it could: it sold the team to an American investment firm for around 150 million euros.

**[DOC]** Headline: "Williams family to leave F1 after team sale to Dorilton Capital" [source: BBC/Autosport, Aug 2020]. The price gets highlighted in yellow.

**[CLIP]** Claire Williams, emotional, in her final weekend interview (Sky Sports F1 / Italian GP 2020). Paraphrase: saying goodbye to the team her father built. *Use her exact words.*

**VO:** It looked like the end of an era. It was actually the bottom of the market.

**[GFX]** Hard cut to black. A single price tag reads **$180M**. It spins over and the back reads **$3,000,000,000+**.

**VO:** Five years later, the most valuable Formula 1 teams are reportedly worth more than three billion dollars each. [VERIFY: latest Forbes/Sportico] Investors are now paying hundreds of millions just for a *slice* of teams that, a decade ago, couldn't pay their bills.

**VO:** So what happened? How did a sport that was bleeding money become one of the hottest investments in the world?

**VO:** To understand it, you have to go back to the man who owned the whole thing.

**[TITLE CARD]** *HOW FORMULA 1 LEARNED TO PRINT MONEY*, with the F1 logo cutout and a checkered paper strip.

## CHAPTER 1: "BERNIE'S EMPIRE" (1:30 – 3:30)

**[CUTOUT]** Bernie Ecclestone, a small figure on a giant stack of 1980s TV sets.

**VO:** For forty years, Formula 1 was essentially run by one man: Bernie Ecclestone, a former used-car dealer from south-east England.

**VO:** In the 1970s, the teams were chaotic, broke and constantly arguing with the circuits about money. Bernie offered them a deal: *let me negotiate for all of you*.

**[GFX]** Ten team logos in a circle. Arrows show each one negotiating separately, then they all get funneled through one Bernie cutout.

**VO:** He bundled every race into a single package and sold it to television networks around the world. Nobody had really done that in motorsport before. And he quietly made sure that the *commercial rights* (the TV money, the race contracts, the trackside advertising) ended up controlled by his companies.

**[DOC]** Concorde Agreement front page (archive), with "commercial rights" highlighted.

**VO:** Here's the business model in one picture.

**[GFX]** Split screen. Left: TEAMS, the people spending hundreds of millions building cars. Right: THE RIGHTS HOLDER, the people collecting the TV, hosting and sponsorship money. Thick gold arrows flow right. A thin trickle flows back left.

**VO:** The teams did the expensive part. The owner of the rights collected the money. And Bernie was very, very good at collecting.

**[CLIP]** Ecclestone's 2014 interview in which he dismissed chasing young fans and social media, saying young people can't afford the luxury brands that sponsor F1 (Campaign Asia, 2014). *Source the exact quote.* [VERIFY]

**VO:** That attitude would come back to haunt the sport. But first, Bernie sold it.

## CHAPTER 2: "THE PRIVATE EQUITY YEARS" (3:30 – 5:30)

**[CUTOUT]** A bank-vault door with the CVC Capital Partners wordmark.

**VO:** In 2006, the private equity firm CVC Capital Partners bought control of Formula 1. CVC did what private equity does: it borrowed heavily against the business, raised prices wherever it could, and paid itself large dividends. [VERIFY: reported debt and dividend totals]

**[GFX]** A money conveyor belt: F1 revenue goes in and dividends drop out into a CVC vault. A debt meter fills up in red.

**VO:** Race hosting fees rose. TV deals moved from free-to-air channels behind pay walls. That meant more money in the short term and fewer new fans in the long term.

**[GFX]** A world map with free-TV countries dimming one by one as they flip to pay-TV.

**VO:** Meanwhile, the teams were stuck in an arms race. There was no limit on spending, and the fastest way to win was to outspend everyone.

**[GFX]** Bar chart race of estimated team budgets, 2010–2019. The top three pull away past $400M. [VERIFY: estimates from team filings]

**VO:** For the big manufacturers, like Mercedes, Ferrari and Red Bull, that was fine. For everyone else, it was a death sentence.

**[CUTOUT]** Three small team cars: HRT, Caterham, Manor. Each gets a red "BANKRUPT" rubber stamp, with a thud sound effect.

**VO:** Between 2012 and 2017, three teams collapsed. Others survived on pay drivers, whose families or sponsors paid for the seat.

**VO:** And the prize money wasn't shared evenly. Ferrari reportedly received a "heritage" payment of tens of millions a year just for being Ferrari. [VERIFY]

**[CUTOUT]** The Ferrari logo on a velvet cushion, with a stack of bills under it.

**VO:** The rich teams got richer. The poor teams went bust. And the owner collected the rent. By 2016, the sport's TV audience was falling. [VERIFY] It needed someone new.

## CHAPTER 3: "THE $8 BILLION BET" (5:30 – 7:30)

**[CUTOUT]** A Liberty Media press release. Chase Carey (with his famous moustache) and Greg Maffei slide in.

**VO:** In January 2017, an American company called Liberty Media completed its takeover of Formula 1, in a deal that valued the business at around eight billion dollars including debt.

**[DOC]** Liberty Media press release, Jan 2017, with "$8.0 billion" highlighted.

**VO:** Liberty is run by people who think about sport the way Americans do: as media. They own the Atlanta Braves baseball team and a big stake in SiriusXM radio. When they looked at Formula 1, they saw something bizarre.

**[GFX]** A phone screen. Under the old rules, teams and drivers were heavily restricted from posting race footage. A "NO VIDEO" sign sits over the F1 logo.

**VO:** A global sport with hundreds of millions of fans… that barely existed on the internet.

**[CLIP]** Chase Carey, 2017, on Liberty's plan to grow F1 as a media and entertainment brand. *Source the exact quote.*

**VO:** Bernie Ecclestone was moved aside. Liberty opened the doors: highlights went on YouTube, teams were allowed on social media, and a streaming service, F1 TV, launched.

**[GFX]** The social follower line starts to tilt upward. [VERIFY: follower counts 2017 → 2024]

**VO:** But the thing that truly changed Formula 1 wasn't a race, or a rule, or a car. It was a television show.

## CHAPTER 4: "DRIVE TO SURVIVE" (7:30 – 9:30)

**[CUTOUT]** The Netflix logo. Drive to Survive key art on a torn poster.

**VO:** In 2019, Netflix released *Formula 1: Drive to Survive*. It didn't really follow the races. It followed the *people*: the rivalries, the egos, the swearing.

**[CLIP]** Guenther Steiner, Haas team principal, from Season 1: his famous blunt, expletive-laden reaction to his team's disastrous race. *Use the actual Netflix clip, bleeped.*

**VO:** Guenther Steiner, the manager of a small American team, became a genuine celebrity. [VERIFY: he later wrote a best-selling book]

**[CUTOUT]** Steiner, Toto Wolff and Christian Horner arranged like a reality-TV cast poster.

**VO:** The show did something no amount of racing could: it gave new fans characters to care about *before* they understood the sport.

**[GFX]** A US map. Viewership rises in bars across successive seasons. [VERIFY: ESPN average US race audience 2018 vs 2022]

**VO:** And America, the one market F1 had never cracked, suddenly fell in love.

**[ARCHIVE]** Miami GP 2022, the Las Vegas Strip in 2023, and celebrities on the grid.

**VO:** Within a few years the calendar had three American races: Austin, Miami and Las Vegas. In Las Vegas, Liberty didn't just sell the race. It *became* the promoter, building a permanent pit building on the Strip. [VERIFY: reported investment of $500M+]

**[CUTOUT]** The Las Vegas pit building photo with a price tag hanging off it.

## CHAPTER 5: "THE CAP THAT CREATED FRANCHISES" (9:30 – 11:30)

**[GFX]** A red horizontal line slams down across the screen: **BUDGET CAP 2021**.

**VO:** In 2021, Formula 1 introduced something that would have been unthinkable in the Ecclestone era: a spending limit. The budget cap started at 145 million dollars a year. [VERIFY: 2026 reset to ~$215M under new definitions]

**VO:** Driver salaries and the top executives' pay are excluded, but almost everything that makes a car fast is inside the cap.

**[GFX]** Team spending bars shoot up, hit the red ceiling and get sliced off. The cut-off pieces fall like coins.

**VO:** Overnight, the economics of owning a team flipped. Before, a team was a cost center. The only way to be competitive was to burn money. Now costs were capped, prize money was growing, and there were only ten seats at the table.

**[GFX]** Side-by-side cutouts of an NFL franchise and an F1 team. The same three icons appear under both: capped costs, shared revenue, limited spots.

**VO:** That's exactly how American sports franchises work. And franchises go up in value.

**[CUTOUT]** Zak Brown with the McLaren papaya car.

**VO:** Look at McLaren. In 2020, during the pandemic, it was borrowing money and selling its own headquarters to survive. [VERIFY: sale-and-leaseback of McLaren Technology Centre, 2021] Its racing team took outside investment from American investors. [VERIFY: MSP Sports Capital] A few years later, McLaren was winning championships again. [VERIFY: 2024 constructors' title]

**[CLIP]** Zak Brown on how the cost cap changed the business case for owning a team. *Source the exact quote.*

**VO:** Now the price of *entry* tells the story. New teams have to pay an anti-dilution fee, compensation to the existing teams for shrinking their share of the prize money. [VERIFY: $200M base] When General Motors' Cadillac team joined for 2026, it reportedly paid far more. [VERIFY]

**[CUTOUT]** The Cadillac logo pushing through a door marked "10 SEATS ONLY".

## CHAPTER 6: "FOLLOW THE MONEY" (11:30 – 13:30)

**VO:** So where does all this money actually come from?

**[GFX]** The F1 hub circle. Three streams feed in, as gold coins flowing along curved paths:
1. **Race promotion fees:** what circuits and governments pay to host a race.
2. **Media rights:** TV and streaming deals.
3. **Sponsorship and other:** global partners, VIP hospitality, licensing.

**VO:** In 2024, Formula 1 brought in about 3.4 billion dollars. [Liberty 10-K] Roughly a third comes from each stream.

**[GFX]** The revenue bar chart from 2017 to 2024, with the COVID dip in red and 2024 in green.

**VO:** Then roughly half of what's left after costs gets paid back to the teams as prize money, under the Concorde Agreement. [VERIFY: 2023 team payments ≈ $1.2B]

**[GFX]** The hub pours into ten buckets, each with a team logo. The buckets are much closer in size than in the Bernie era.

**VO:** But the most controversial number is the hosting fee. Classic European tracks pay the least. The highest fees, reportedly 50 to 60 million dollars a year, come from governments in the Gulf. [VERIFY]

**[CUTOUT]** A calendar wall. Each race is a paper tile whose height equals its hosting fee. The Gulf tiles tower over Monza and Silverstone.

**VO:** Critics call it sportswashing: countries using a glamorous sport to improve their image. F1 argues it brings investment and attention.

**[CLIP]** Human rights organisation statement (Amnesty/HRW), then an F1 or FIA response. *Present both sides.*

## CHAPTER 7: "WHO WON?" (13:30 – 15:00)

**VO:** So who actually wins in the new Formula 1?

**[GFX]** A podium. Each step gets a cutout.

**VO:** **Liberty Media's shareholders:** they bought F1 for about eight billion dollars. It's now worth several times that. [VERIFY: market value of F1 tracking stock / FWONK]

**VO:** **The teams:** former money pits have become some of the most valuable assets in sport.

**VO:** **The drivers:** their salaries sit *outside* the cap, and the best are paid tens of millions a year. [VERIFY]

**VO:** And the fans? They got more races, more drama and more access. They also got more expensive tickets, and a sport where Saturday night in Las Vegas can cost more than a month's rent. [VERIFY: ticket prices]

**[ARCHIVE]** Back to Monza, 2020. The Williams garage.

**VO:** Remember Williams? The team that sold for about 150 million euros at the very bottom of the market? Its new owners have poured money in, hired a former Mercedes strategist as boss, and signed a world-class driver. [VERIFY: James Vowles; Carlos Sainz 2025]

**[CUTOUT]** James Vowles and Carlos Sainz beside the modern Williams car. A price tag spins up: **$180M → ?**

**VO:** For fifty years, the fastest cars in the world were attached to one of the slowest-moving businesses in sport. It took an American media company, a reality show and a spending limit to make Formula 1 do the one thing it had never managed…

**VO:** …turn speed into profit.
