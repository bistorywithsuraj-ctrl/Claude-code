## 1. THE MONEY PIT (1:12 – 3:30)

**VO:**
To understand how strange this turnaround is, you have to understand how Formula 1 used to work, and who it used to work *for*.

For most of its modern history, F1 was really two businesses bolted together. On one side were the teams: ten or so companies that design, build and race the cars. On the other was the company that owns the *commercial rights*: the TV deals, the race contracts and the trackside advertising.

The teams did the expensive part. The rights holder collected most of the money.

From the late 1970s on, that rights business was built and run by one man: Bernie Ecclestone. He worked out, before almost anyone else in sport, that the valuable thing wasn't the race. It was the *broadcast* of the race. He bundled the TV rights for every Grand Prix, sold them as a package, and took a cut of everything.

In 2006 the private equity firm CVC Capital Partners bought control of F1. [VERIFY: price, commonly cited as ~$1.7B + debt] CVC ran it as a cash machine: it charged high hosting fees, sold TV rights to pay-TV networks, loaded the company with debt, and paid large dividends to its owners.

**Visual:** split-screen "TEAMS | RIGHTS HOLDER". Money arrows flow almost entirely to the right. Illustrated Ecclestone silhouette (no photo likeness) next to a TV antenna. CVC rendered as a vault with a dividend conveyor.

Meanwhile the teams were in an arms race. With no limit on spending, the only way to win was to outspend everyone. The big manufacturer teams spent hundreds of millions a year. Smaller teams borrowed, begged, and sometimes simply disappeared. [List: HRT, Caterham, Manor, all gone in the 2010s.]

**Visual:** tombstones labeled with defunct team names on a grid; red "✕" stamps.

And the prize money wasn't shared evenly. Legacy deals gave extra payments to certain teams just for existing. Ferrari received a "historic" bonus [VERIFY: reported ~$70M+/yr] before a single car turned a wheel.

**Beat line:** *The richest teams got richer. The poorest ones went bust. And the sport's owner collected the rent.*

## 2. THE $8 BILLION BET (3:30 – 6:00)

**VO:**
In 2017 an American company called Liberty Media, which owns the Atlanta Braves and a big slice of SiriusXM, bought Formula 1 in a deal that valued it at about eight billion dollars, including debt.

Liberty looked at F1 and saw something odd: a global sport with hundreds of millions of fans that barely used the internet. Under the old regime, teams and drivers were restricted from posting race footage on social media. Almost nothing reached younger audiences, and almost nothing reached America.

So Liberty did three things:
1. **Opened the doors:** social media, YouTube highlights, F1 TV.
2. **Said yes to Netflix.** *Drive to Survive* launched in 2019 and turned team principals and drivers into reality-TV characters. [VERIFY: US viewership growth figures]
3. **Went after America:** Miami (2022) and Las Vegas (2023), where Liberty acted as its own race promoter.

**Visual:** a US map lights up with three pins (Austin, Miami, Las Vegas). The follower-count line (social) hockey-sticks after 2019.

## 3. WHERE THE MONEY COMES FROM (6:00 – 9:00)

**VO:**
Formula 1's income, what Liberty calls "primary revenue", comes from three main places.

| Stream | What it is | Rough share **[VERIFY latest year]** |
|---|---|---|
| **Race promotion fees** | Circuits and governments pay to host a Grand Prix | ~ ⅓ |
| **Media rights** | TV and streaming deals worldwide, plus F1 TV | ~ ⅓ |
| **Sponsorship & other** | Global partners, hospitality (Paddock Club), licensing, freight | ~ ⅓ |

**Hosting fees deep-dive:** traditional European tracks pay far less than new state-backed races. Figures reported in the press range from roughly **$20M** to about **$55–60M a year** at the top end [VERIFY]. That's why the calendar has shifted toward the Middle East and the Americas.

**Visual:** a 24-tile calendar where each tile's height is the hosting fee, color-coded by region. The old-school circuits sit low, the Gulf races tower.

## 4. WHERE THE MONEY GOES (9:00 – 11:30)

**VO:**
Here's the part most fans never see. After costs, a big chunk of F1's profit gets paid back to the teams as prize money. That pool is set by the Concorde Agreement, the contract between F1, the FIA and the teams. [VERIFY: 2023 team payments ≈ $1.2B+]

The payout is now far flatter than in the Ecclestone era. There is still a gap between first and last, and Ferrari still gets its heritage payment, but the backmarkers can survive now.

**Visual:** a prize-money "waterfall": the F1 hub pours into 10 buckets of slightly different sizes. Then a before/after comparison of the gap between the top and bottom team.

## 5. THE CAP THAT CREATED FRANCHISES (11:30 – 14:00)

**VO:**
In 2021, F1 introduced something unthinkable a decade earlier: a **budget cap**. It started at $145M, came down to $135M, and was reset to around $215M for 2026 under new, broader accounting rules [VERIFY]. Driver salaries and the top three executives' pay are excluded.

Overnight, the math of owning a team flipped. Before, a team was a cost center: a marketing expense for a car company. Now the costs were capped, prize money was rising, and there were only ten spots on the grid.

That is exactly how an American sports franchise works: **fixed costs, shared revenue, scarcity.** Investors noticed. New entrants have to pay an anti-dilution fee to join [VERIFY: $200M base; Cadillac/GM's 2026 entry reportedly paid more], because every new team shrinks everyone else's slice.

**Visual:** a line chart of team valuations, from Williams at ~$180M (2020) to $3B+ leaders (2025). NFL/NBA franchise icons slide in next to F1 as "the same shape".

---

## 6. THE CITIES & THE CONTROVERSY (14:00 – 16:00)

**VO:**
Why would a city pay tens of millions to close its streets for a weekend? Tourism, TV exposure, and increasingly, image. Several of the highest fees come from Gulf states, which has sparked a debate about "sportswashing" that F1 has never fully answered.

Las Vegas is the other extreme: Liberty became its own promoter and built a permanent pit building on the Strip [VERIFY: reported $500M+ investment]. It's a bet that F1 can sell a *spectacle*, not just a race.

---

## 7. WHO WINS? (16:00 – 17:30) + OUTRO

**VO:**
So who actually keeps the money?
- **Liberty's shareholders:** F1's value has multiplied since the 2017 deal.
- **The teams:** once money pits, now appreciating assets.
- **The drivers:** outside the cap, with salaries in the tens of millions.
- **The fans** pay more for everything, from tickets to streaming.

**Closing line:** *For fifty years, the fastest cars in the world were attached to one of the slowest businesses in sport. It took an American media company, a reality show and a spending limit to make Formula 1 do the one thing it had never managed: turn speed into profit.*
