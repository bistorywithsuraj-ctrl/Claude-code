## 1. THE SLUM SPORT (1:30 – 3:45)

**VO:**
In the 1980s, English football was in trouble. Not "losing a bit of money" trouble. Existential trouble.

Attendances had fallen for two decades. Stadiums were crumbling terraces with fences to keep fans off the pitch. Hooliganism was a national embarrassment. In 1985, a fire at Bradford killed 56 people. A few weeks later, rioting at the European Cup final in Brussels killed 39. English clubs were banned from European competition for five years. [VERIFY dates and counts]

A newspaper editorial that year called football "a slum sport, played in slum stadiums, watched by slum people." [VERIFY: Sunday Times, May 1985]

Then, in April 1989, 97 Liverpool supporters were crushed to death at Hillsborough. [VERIFY: count revised to 97 in 2021] The inquiry that followed, the Taylor Report, ordered every top-division ground to become all-seater. That cost money. And the clubs didn't have any.

**Visual:** an attendance line chart, 1949 peak to the 1985 trough. Photo cutouts of terraces and fences, grayscale. The Taylor Report front page on paper texture, "all-seated" highlighted.

Television wasn't paying much either. In 1988, ITV bought the rights to the entire First Division for about £44 million over four years: roughly £11 million a season for the whole league. [VERIFY] For comparison, one mid-table club now gets ten times that from TV every year.

**Beat line:** *The biggest clubs looked at the money coming in, looked at how it was being shared with 90 other clubs, and decided they wanted out.*

## 2. THE BREAKAWAY (3:45 – 6:15)

**VO:**
The "Big Five" (Manchester United, Liverpool, Arsenal, Everton and Tottenham) had been plotting since a dinner in 1988. [VERIFY: Greg Dyke, London Weekend Television] The idea was simple: split from the Football League, keep the TV money, and share it among the top clubs only.

In 1992, they did it. Twenty-two clubs formed the FA Premier League. Same players, same stadiums, new company. The one thing that actually changed was who owned the television rights.

And that mattered because of a satellite company that was, at the time, nearly bankrupt.

BSkyB had lost hundreds of millions launching pay-TV in Britain. Rupert Murdoch needed something people would pay for. His later line was that sport would be "a battering ram" for pay television. [VERIFY: 1996 quote] In May 1992, Sky bid £304 million for five years of live Premier League football. ITV was outbid. The BBC got the highlights.

**Visual:** the 22 club crests peel off a "FOOTBALL LEAGUE" board and reassemble on a "PREMIER LEAGUE" board. A satellite dish cutout. Rights bars: **£44M (ITV, 1988)** next to **£304M (Sky, 1992)**.

It was the first time in Britain that a whole sport disappeared behind a paywall. Fans hated it. Then they subscribed. By the end of the decade Sky had millions of customers and football was the reason. [VERIFY: subscriber figures]

**Beat line:** *The Premier League wasn't a football idea. It was a television idea.*

## 3. THE AUCTION (6:15 – 9:00)

**VO:**
The Premier League does one thing better than any league in the world: it sells itself. Every three or four years it auctions its rights in packages, and it makes broadcasters bid against each other.

| Cycle | Domestic rights (UK) | Per season | **[VERIFY all]** |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1992–97 | £304M | ~£61M | Sky |
| 1997–01 | £670M | ~£168M | Sky |
| 2001–04 | £1.2B | ~£400M | Sky |
| 2007–10 | £1.7B | ~£570M | Sky + Setanta (EU forced a second bidder) |
| 2013–16 | £3.0B | ~£1.0B | Sky + BT enter |
| 2016–19 | £5.1B | ~£1.7B | Sky + BT, the peak per season |
| 2019–22 | £4.5B | ~£1.5B | first dip; Amazon buys a small package |
| 2025–29 | £6.7B | ~£1.7B | Sky + TNT, 270 live matches, longest deal yet |

The domestic market has flattened. Growth now comes from abroad. For the 2022–25 cycle, international rights (about £5.3 billion) were worth more than domestic rights for the first time. [VERIFY] The US deal alone, with NBC, is worth about $2.7 billion over six years. [VERIFY: 2022–28]

**Visual:** the bar chart above, with an international bar overtaking the domestic one in 2022. A world map with time zones: 3pm Saturday in England is prime time in Asia and breakfast in America.

**Why other leagues can't copy it:** the Premier League sells collectively and shares more equally than any rival. In the 2023–24 season the champions received about £175 million from the league; the bottom club still got about £110 million. [VERIFY] That ratio, roughly 1.6 to 1, is the flattest in Europe. In Spain, until recently, Real Madrid and Barcelona negotiated their own deals. [VERIFY]

**Visual:** ten equal-ish bars in green (Premier League) next to a lopsided pair (La Liga, pre-2015).

**Beat line:** *Twenty clubs. One seller. Everybody gets rich, even the losers.*

## 4. THE OWNERS (9:00 – 11:30)

**VO:**
Where there's guaranteed money, buyers follow. And the buyers changed the game three times.

**2003, the billionaire.** Roman Abramovich bought Chelsea and spent whatever it took. Losses didn't matter; the owner covered them. Chelsea won the league in 2005 for the first time in 50 years. [VERIFY] The lesson every club learned: to compete, find a billionaire.

**2005, the leveraged buyout.** The Glazer family bought Manchester United for about £790 million, and borrowed most of it, then loaded the debt onto the club. [VERIFY: ~£525M borrowed] United has since paid more than a billion pounds in interest, fees and dividends. [VERIFY: press estimates] Fans protested for 20 years. The club is still worth about $6 billion. [VERIFY: Forbes]

**2008, the state.** Sheikh Mansour of Abu Dhabi bought Manchester City for about £210 million. [VERIFY] In 2021 Saudi Arabia's sovereign wealth fund bought Newcastle for about £305 million. [VERIFY] Owners with, effectively, unlimited money.

**Visual:** three doors labelled BILLIONAIRE · DEBT · STATE. A cutout walks through each. A grid of 20 crests recolours: gold for foreign owners, and about half of them US-owned by 2025. [VERIFY count]

And now, the Americans. Liverpool (Fenway), Arsenal (Kroenke), Chelsea (Clearlake), Manchester United (Ratcliffe's INEOS with the Glazers), Aston Villa, Bournemouth, Crystal Palace, Fulham, Leeds, Ipswich, Everton… [VERIFY list] American investors who understand exactly why this league is valuable: it's the NFL of the rest of the world, with one enormous exception.

**Beat line:** *There is no salary cap. There is no draft. And every year three clubs fall off a cliff.*

## 5. THE ARMS RACE (11:30 – 13:45)

**VO:**
Here's the paradox from the top of the episode.

In the 2022–23 season, Premier League clubs made a record £6.1 billion in revenue. Combined, they lost about £685 million before tax. [VERIFY: Deloitte Annual Review of Football Finance 2024] Only a handful of clubs made a profit.

Where did the money go? Players.

**Visual:** the revenue bar (green) with a wages bar (red) that eats most of it, then a transfers/amortisation bar that eats the rest and dips below zero.

Wages take about two-thirds of everything the clubs earn. [VERIFY] Then there are transfer fees: in the summer of 2023, Premier League clubs spent about £2.4 billion on players, more than the other four big European leagues combined. [VERIFY] And the agents: over £400 million in one season. [VERIFY]

Why spend it all? Because the money is guaranteed only if you stay up. Relegation costs a club roughly £100 million a year in lost TV money, softened by "parachute payments" for three seasons. [VERIFY amounts] So every club spends to the limit to avoid the cliff, and the clubs at the top spend to the limit to reach the Champions League. Nobody can afford to stop.

**Visual:** the "cliff": 20 green bars, then a drop to 3 red bars in the Championship, with parachute umbrellas shrinking over three years.

**Beat line:** *In Formula 1, the cap made teams profitable. In football, the cliff makes them spend.*

## 6. THE CAP THAT ISN'T (13:45 – 15:45)

**VO:**
The league's answer is a set of accounting rules, not a spending cap. Under the Profit and Sustainability Rules, a club may lose up to £105 million over three seasons. [VERIFY] Break it and you lose points.

In 2023–24, for the first time, the league actually did it: Everton were docked points twice, Nottingham Forest once. [VERIFY: 6 + 2, and 4] Manchester City faced more than a hundred charges of breaking financial rules over nine seasons; the hearing ran through late 2024. [VERIFY the outcome and state it on screen as of the recording date]

**Visual:** the £105M line on a loss chart. Everton and Forest bars dip under it and get a red **−6**, **−2**, **−4**. A "115" folder for City with a **[VERIFY OUTCOME]** sticker.

Clubs have found ways around it. Chelsea's new owners signed players on eight-year contracts to spread the cost, until UEFA and the league capped contracts at five years. [VERIFY] Chelsea then sold two hotels, and later its women's team, to companies owned by its own owners, and booked the profit. [VERIFY: £76.5M and ~£200M, from Chelsea FC Holdings accounts]

Now the league is moving toward a "squad cost ratio", where spending on players is limited to a share of revenue, and it has debated "anchoring" spending to a multiple of what the bottom club earns. [VERIFY current status] And in 2025 Parliament created an independent regulator for English football. [VERIFY: Football Governance Act 2025]

**Beat line:** *The Premier League has rules against losing money. It just doesn't have rules against spending it.*

## 7. THE FRANCHISE THAT WASN'T (15:45 – 17:00)

**VO:**
The obvious fix, if you're an American owner, is to do what Formula 1 did: close the league. No relegation, fixed costs, guaranteed revenue. Franchises.

In April 2021, twelve clubs, including six from England, tried it. The European Super League, financed with more than $4 billion from JPMorgan. [VERIFY] Permanent members, no relegation.

It lasted about 48 hours. Fans blocked the team bus outside Stamford Bridge. The government threatened to legislate. Players and managers turned on their own owners. All six English clubs withdrew. [VERIFY timeline]

**Visual:** a Super League "franchise contract" on paper. Fans' protest cutouts. The contract tears in half, stop-motion.

**Beat line:** *The thing that makes the Premier League less profitable than the NFL, the risk of falling, is also the thing that makes people watch.*

## 8. WHO WINS? (17:00 – 18:00) + OUTRO

**VO:**
So who keeps the money?
- **The owners:** clubs bought for hundreds of millions are worth billions, as long as they never have to sell in a hurry.
- **The players and their agents:** two-thirds of every pound.
- **The broadcasters:** Sky built a business on it, and NBC turned Saturday morning into a habit.
- **The fans** pay more for everything: tickets, two or three subscriptions, and a 3pm Saturday blackout that has lasted since the 1960s. [VERIFY]

Back to Chelsea. The £2.5 billion Abramovich was paid still sits frozen in a British bank account, promised to Ukraine, three years on. [VERIFY status] The new owners spent more than a billion pounds on players in their first two years. [VERIFY] They've won a European trophy and a Club World Cup since. [VERIFY: 2025] And, like almost everyone else in this league, they lose money doing it.

**Closing line:** *Formula 1 learned to print money by becoming a private club. English football did the opposite: it stayed open, stayed uncapped and kept losing money, and the world paid billions to watch it. The Premier League isn't the best-run league in the world. It's the best-sold one.*
