# HOW THE PREMIER LEAGUE TOOK OVER THE WORLD
### A Greenback documentary · Episode 2 · ~17 minutes · Vox-style

**Format key**
- **VO** = narration (≈145 words/min).
- **[CLIP]** = a real interview or archive moment to source. Lines marked "paraphrase" describe what the person says and must be replaced with their exact words from the source clip. Never invent a quote.
- **[CUTOUT]** = a real photo, background removed, with a white paper edge, drop shadow and slight parallax.
- **[DOC]** = a real document, headline or filing on paper texture, with a highlighter pass.
- **[GFX]** = animated infographic.
- **[VERIFY]** = confirm the figure before recording.

## COLD OPEN: "THE FROZEN CLUB" (0:00 – 1:30)

**[ARCHIVE]** Stamford Bridge, 10 March 2022. The club shop with its shutters down. A handwritten "closed until further notice" sign.

**VO:** On a Thursday in March 2022, the British government froze the assets of a football club.

**[CUTOUT]** Roman Abramovich, grayscale, on paper. Behind him, a halftone of the 2003 back page: "Russian buys Chelsea".

**VO:** Nineteen years earlier a Russian billionaire, Roman Abramovich, had bought Chelsea for around one hundred and forty million pounds and turned it into a trophy machine: five league titles, two Champions Leagues, more than twenty trophies. [VERIFY]

**[GFX]** Trophy counters tick up on green cards: **5** · **2** · **21**.

**VO:** Now it couldn't sell a ticket. It couldn't sell a shirt. It couldn't sign a player. And its owner had ninety days to sell it, without keeping a penny.

**[DOC]** Headline: "Chelsea sold to Todd Boehly-led consortium in £4.25bn deal" [source: BBC / Reuters, May 2022]. **£2.5 billion** highlighted in yellow.

**[CLIP]** Thomas Tuchel, press conference, 10 March 2022 (Chelsea FC / Sky Sports News), on running a team under sanctions. *Use his exact words.*

**VO:** A forced sale, in a war, at the worst possible moment. It still fetched the highest price ever paid for a football club: two and a half billion pounds. About eighteen times what Abramovich paid.

**[GFX]** A hanging price tag reads **£140M**. It flips over: **£2.5B**. **×18** in highlighter.

**VO:** Here's the strange part. Most Premier League clubs lose money. Every year. So why is the league that loses money the most valuable league on Earth?

**[GFX]** A pin board: the logos of the people paying. Sky, TNT, NBC, Amazon Prime, DAZN, Paramount+, Nike, Adidas, Emirates, EA.

**VO:** To understand it, you have to go back to the year English football nearly died.

**[TITLE CARD]** *HOW THE PREMIER LEAGUE TOOK OVER THE WORLD*, with the Premier League lion cutout and a checkered paper strip.

## CHAPTER 1: "THE SLUM SPORT" (1:30 – 3:45)

**[ARCHIVE]** 1980s terraces. Fences. Rain. Grayscale, paper edges.

**VO:** In the 1980s, English football was in trouble. Not "losing a bit of money" trouble. Existential trouble.

**[GFX]** Attendance line, 1949 to 1985, sliding downhill. [VERIFY: Football League aggregate attendance]

**VO:** Attendances had been falling for two decades. Stadiums were crumbling terraces with fences to keep fans off the pitch. Hooliganism was a national embarrassment. In 1985 a fire at Bradford killed fifty-six people. Weeks later, rioting at the European Cup final in Brussels killed thirty-nine, and English clubs were banned from Europe for five years. [VERIFY]

**[DOC]** Sunday Times editorial, May 1985: football as "a slum sport played in slum stadiums watched by slum people". Highlight the phrase. [VERIFY exact wording]

**VO:** In April 1989, ninety-seven Liverpool supporters were crushed to death at Hillsborough. [VERIFY count] The inquiry that followed ordered every top-division ground to become all-seater. That cost money. And the clubs didn't have any.

**[DOC]** The Taylor Report cover (January 1990), "all-seated" highlighted.

**VO:** Television wasn't paying much either. In 1988, ITV bought the whole First Division for about forty-four million pounds over four years. [VERIFY] Roughly eleven million a season for the entire league.

**[GFX]** A tiny bar: **£11M / season**. A ghost bar beside it labelled "one mid-table club, today": ten times taller.

**VO:** The biggest clubs looked at the money coming in, looked at how it was shared with ninety other clubs, and decided they wanted out.

## CHAPTER 2: "THE BREAKAWAY" (3:45 – 6:15)

**[CUTOUT]** Five club crests around a restaurant table (the 1988 dinner). Greg Dyke of London Weekend Television at the head. [VERIFY]

**VO:** The "Big Five", Manchester United, Liverpool, Arsenal, Everton and Tottenham, had been plotting since a dinner in 1988. The idea was simple: split from the Football League, keep the television money, and share it among the top clubs only.

**VO:** In 1992, they did it. Twenty-two clubs formed the FA Premier League. Same players, same stadiums, new company. The one thing that changed was who owned the television rights.

**[GFX]** Twenty-two crests peel off a "FOOTBALL LEAGUE" board and re-pin themselves on a "PREMIER LEAGUE" board.

**VO:** And that mattered because of a satellite company that was, at the time, nearly bankrupt.

**[CUTOUT]** A satellite dish. Rupert Murdoch. A BSkyB logo on a torn poster.

**VO:** BSkyB had lost hundreds of millions launching pay television in Britain. Rupert Murdoch needed something people would pay for. He'd later describe sport as "a battering ram" for pay TV. [VERIFY: 1996 News Corp AGM] In May 1992, Sky bid three hundred and four million pounds for five years of live Premier League football. ITV was outbid. The BBC got the highlights.

**[GFX]** Two bars: **£44M (ITV, 1988–92)** and **£304M (Sky, 1992–97)**. The second one keeps growing off the top of the frame.

**[CLIP]** Archive: the first Sky Sports Premier League broadcast, August 1992 ("A whole new ball game"). *Fair-use excerpt.*

**VO:** It was the first time in Britain that a whole sport disappeared behind a paywall. Fans hated it. Then they subscribed. By the end of the decade Sky had millions of customers, and football was the reason. [VERIFY]

**VO:** The Premier League wasn't a football idea. It was a television idea.

## CHAPTER 3: "THE AUCTION" (6:15 – 9:00)

**VO:** The Premier League does one thing better than any league in the world: it sells itself.

**[GFX]** The rights-cycle bar chart, 1992 to 2029, in green. Each bar labelled with the broadcaster. The 2019 dip in red. [VERIFY every figure]

**VO:** Every three or four years it auctions its matches in packages and makes broadcasters bid against each other. Three hundred million became six hundred and seventy million, became one point two billion, became three billion, became five billion. The latest domestic deal is six point seven billion pounds over four years. [VERIFY]

**VO:** The home market has flattened. Growth now comes from abroad. For the 2022 to 2025 cycle, international rights were worth more than domestic rights for the first time. [VERIFY: ~£5.3B vs ~£5.1B] The American deal alone, with NBC, is worth about two point seven billion dollars over six years. [VERIFY]

**[GFX]** A world map with clocks: 3pm Saturday in London is 11pm in Singapore and 10am in New York. The whole map lights up green.

**[CLIP]** NBC Sports / Rebecca Lowe or Peter Drury on the American audience for the Premier League. *Source the exact quote.*

**VO:** And here's why other leagues can't copy it. The Premier League sells everything collectively and shares it more equally than any rival. In the 2023–24 season the champions received about a hundred and seventy-five million pounds from the league. The club that finished last still got about a hundred and ten million. [VERIFY]

**[GFX]** Twenty green bars, nearly level. Beside them, La Liga before 2015: two enormous bars and eighteen stubs. [VERIFY]

**VO:** Twenty clubs. One seller. Everybody gets rich, even the losers.

## CHAPTER 4: "THE OWNERS" (9:00 – 11:30)

**VO:** Where there's guaranteed money, buyers follow. And the buyers changed the game three times.

**[CUTOUT]** Three paper doors: BILLIONAIRE · DEBT · STATE.

**VO:** Two thousand and three, the billionaire. Roman Abramovich bought Chelsea and spent whatever it took. Losses didn't matter, the owner covered them. Chelsea won the league in 2005, for the first time in fifty years. [VERIFY] Every club learned the same lesson: to compete, find a billionaire.

**[CLIP]** José Mourinho, 2004, "I think I am a special one". *Fair-use excerpt.*

**VO:** Two thousand and five, the leveraged buyout. The Glazer family bought Manchester United for about seven hundred and ninety million pounds, borrowed most of it, and put the debt on the club. [VERIFY] United has since paid more than a billion pounds in interest, fees and dividends. [VERIFY: press estimates] Fans have protested for twenty years. The club is still worth around six billion dollars. [VERIFY: Forbes]

**[DOC]** A Manchester United plc filing (NYSE), the interest line highlighted.

**VO:** Two thousand and eight, the state. Sheikh Mansour of Abu Dhabi bought Manchester City for about two hundred and ten million pounds. In 2021, Saudi Arabia's sovereign wealth fund bought Newcastle for about three hundred and five million. [VERIFY] Owners with, in effect, unlimited money.

**[GFX]** A grid of twenty crests recolours by owner nationality. About half turn stars-and-stripes. [VERIFY count as of the recording date]

**VO:** And now, the Americans. Liverpool, Arsenal, Chelsea, Manchester United, Aston Villa, Bournemouth, Crystal Palace, Fulham… [VERIFY list] Investors who understand exactly why this league is valuable: it's the NFL of the rest of the world. With one enormous exception.

**VO:** There is no salary cap. There is no draft. And every year, three clubs fall off a cliff.

## CHAPTER 5: "THE ARMS RACE" (11:30 – 13:45)

**VO:** Here's the paradox from the top of the episode.

**[GFX]** Revenue bar in green: **£6.1B** (2022–23). A red wages bar eats two-thirds of it. A transfer-amortisation bar eats the rest and dips below the line: **−£685M**. [VERIFY: Deloitte Annual Review of Football Finance 2024; update to the latest season]

**VO:** In the 2022–23 season Premier League clubs made a record six point one billion pounds. Combined, they lost about six hundred and eighty-five million before tax. Only a handful made a profit.

**VO:** Where did the money go? Players.

**[CUTOUT]** A player cutout, an agent cutout and a stack of contracts.

**VO:** Wages take about two-thirds of everything the clubs earn. [VERIFY] Then transfer fees: in the summer of 2023, Premier League clubs spent about two point four billion pounds on players, more than the other four big European leagues combined. [VERIFY] And agents: over four hundred million pounds in one season. [VERIFY]

**VO:** Why spend it all? Because the money is only guaranteed if you stay up.

**[GFX]** "The cliff": twenty green bars, then a drop to the Championship in red. Three paper umbrellas (parachute payments) shrink over three seasons. [VERIFY amounts]

**VO:** Relegation costs a club roughly a hundred million pounds a year in lost television money, softened by parachute payments for three seasons. So every club spends to the limit to avoid the cliff, and the clubs at the top spend to the limit to reach the Champions League. Nobody can afford to stop.

**VO:** In Formula 1, the cap made teams profitable. In football, the cliff makes them spend.

## CHAPTER 6: "THE CAP THAT ISN'T" (13:45 – 15:45)

**[GFX]** A red line labelled **£105M / 3 seasons** across a loss chart.

**VO:** The league's answer is a set of accounting rules, not a spending cap. Under the Profit and Sustainability Rules a club may lose up to a hundred and five million pounds over three seasons. [VERIFY] Break it and you lose points.

**[GFX]** Everton's bar dips under the line: **−6**, then **−2**. Nottingham Forest: **−4**. [VERIFY]

**VO:** In the 2023–24 season, for the first time, the league actually did it. Everton were docked points twice. Nottingham Forest once.

**[DOC]** The Premier League's charge sheet against Manchester City (February 2023), the number of charges highlighted. A **[VERIFY OUTCOME]** sticker.

**VO:** Manchester City faced more than a hundred charges of breaking financial rules over nine seasons. The hearing ran through late 2024. [State the outcome on screen as of the recording date.]

**VO:** Clubs have found ways around the rules. Chelsea's new owners signed players on eight-year contracts to spread the cost, until UEFA and the league capped contracts at five years. [VERIFY] Chelsea then sold two hotels, and later its women's team, to companies owned by its own owners, and booked the profit. [VERIFY: Chelsea FC Holdings accounts, ~£76.5M and ~£200M]

**[CUTOUT]** A hotel cutout and a women's team shirt, each with a "SOLD (to ourselves)" tag.

**VO:** Now the league is moving toward a squad-cost ratio, where spending on players is limited to a share of revenue, and it has argued about anchoring spending to a multiple of what the bottom club earns. [VERIFY status] And in 2025 Parliament created an independent regulator for English football. [VERIFY: Football Governance Act 2025]

**VO:** The Premier League has rules against losing money. It just doesn't have rules against spending it.

## CHAPTER 7: "THE FRANCHISE THAT WASN'T" (15:45 – 17:00)

**VO:** The obvious fix, if you're an American owner, is to do what Formula 1 did. Close the league. No relegation, fixed costs, guaranteed revenue. Franchises.

**[DOC]** The European Super League announcement, 18 April 2021, "founding clubs" highlighted. The twelve crests. [VERIFY: JPMorgan financing of more than $4B]

**VO:** In April 2021, twelve clubs, six of them English, tried it. The European Super League. Permanent members. No relegation.

**[ARCHIVE]** Chelsea fans outside Stamford Bridge, 20 April 2021, blocking the team bus. Protest banners at Elland Road and Old Trafford.

**VO:** It lasted about forty-eight hours. Fans blocked the team bus outside Stamford Bridge. The government threatened to legislate. Players and managers turned on their own owners. All six English clubs withdrew. [VERIFY timeline]

**[CLIP]** Gary Neville's live reaction on Sky Sports, 18 April 2021. *Fair-use excerpt, exact words.*

**[GFX]** The franchise contract tears in half in stop-motion.

**VO:** The thing that makes the Premier League less profitable than the NFL, the risk of falling, is also the thing that makes people watch.

## CHAPTER 8: "WHO WON?" (17:00 – 18:00)

**[GFX]** A podium. Each step gets a cutout.

**VO:** So who keeps the money? The owners: clubs bought for hundreds of millions are worth billions, as long as they never have to sell in a hurry. The players and their agents: two-thirds of every pound. The broadcasters: Sky built a business on it, and NBC turned Saturday morning into an American habit.

**VO:** And the fans? They pay more for everything: tickets, two or three subscriptions, and a Saturday-afternoon blackout that has lasted since the 1960s. [VERIFY]

**[ARCHIVE]** Back to Stamford Bridge. The shop is open again.

**VO:** Remember Chelsea? The two and a half billion pounds Abramovich was paid still sits frozen in a British bank account, promised to Ukraine, years later. [VERIFY status] The new owners spent more than a billion pounds on players in their first two years. [VERIFY] They've won a European trophy and a Club World Cup since. [VERIFY: 2025] And, like almost everyone else in this league, they lose money doing it.

**[GFX]** The price tag from the cold open swings back in. The counter keeps ticking.

**VO:** Formula 1 learned to print money by becoming a private club. English football did the opposite: it stayed open, stayed uncapped and kept losing money, and the world paid billions to watch it.

**VO:** The Premier League isn't the best-run league in the world. It's the best-sold one.
